Pointing Trail Tater toward Plymouth

Arrival: about 2:10 PM

We pulled into Plymouth at about 2:10 this afternoon. On the ride up, it poured. Then it poured again—and again. As we got closer to Plymouth it stopped, then started once more, and finally stopped long enough for us to build camp.

We got Trail Tater parked and leveled, then set up the tents. Before long the canopy was up, the chairs were gathered around the fire ring, and our little Plymouth camp had taken shape beneath the pines.

The afternoon stayed overcast with a slight breeze. With camp complete, we went in search of food—just like my forefathers did when they came here, although we had a truck and GPS to help us along.

Day Two · Thursday, July 30

Rain, Rocks & Rainbow Flames

Rain settled over Sandy Pond for much of the morning, but it never managed to dampen the Trail Tater spirit. Coffee stayed hot beneath the canopy, breakfast filled the air with the smell of pancakes and bacon, and wild turkeys wandered through camp as though they had come to inspect the newcomers.

What began as a wet day became one of the fullest days of the trip—part campground adventure, part family art project, and part old-fashioned evening around the fire.

Breakfast beneath the canopy

The Blackstone produced pancakes, bacon, and sausage while the rain fell beyond the shelter. The first pancake went to Sade, the newest member of the Trail Tater crew and a first-time Trail Tater adventurer.

Foraging, exploring & Enisketomp

With the rain still shaping the day, the crew headed out for a little modern-day “foraging” at Walmart, then visited Enisketomp—“Human Being”, Peter Wolf Toth's carved tribute to Indigenous people.

Back at Sandy Pond, the girls explored the trails near the pond. It is not the campground's swimming pond, but it gave everyone a chance to stretch their legs, look around, and discover more of the place we were calling home for the week. More turkeys appeared, another camper reported seeing a skunk, and Ken's own wildlife tally included a squirrel—Stewie would have considered that a major sighting.

Rainy-day rock painting

The afternoon's rain brought everyone back beneath the canopy, where the picnic table became an art studio. Paints, brushes, paper plates, and campground stones covered the table as the crew turned ordinary rocks into little pieces of Trail Tater history.

The idea had a lovely beginning: painted rocks had been waiting around the campground when the crew arrived. Rather than simply keeping the surprise, everyone decided to pass it along. When the new rocks were finished, they were hidden around Sandy Pond for future campers to discover.

Billie feeds the crew

As evening approached, Billie took charge of camp kitchen. She handled the kabobs on the grill while cooking corn on the cob on the Blackstone, keeping both stations moving and making sure the entire crew was fed.

She deserves an extra nod for stepping up and turning a damp camping day into a proper family meal. The smell of dinner drifting through camp was every bit as welcome as the clearing sky.

Firemaster takes over

Once dinner was finished, Ken resumed his official role as Firemaster. The fire began small, caught steadily, and soon filled the truck-rim fire ring with a strong evening blaze.

Trail Tater's eye color was put to a crew vote. Red won the night. From now on, everyone gets a vote—and should the count ever tie, the Firemaster holds the deciding ballot.

Rainbow flames

After dinner was safely finished, Rainbow Flame Crystals went into the fire. Blues and greens began appearing beneath the familiar orange flames, growing brighter as darkness settled over the campsite.

The effect turned an already good campfire into the perfect finale for a day that had started with steady rain.

Day Three · Friday, July 31

The bright orb returns

Day Three began with a strange and unfamiliar sight above Sandy Pond: a warm, bright orb peeking through the pines—and not a single droplet of water pummeling us from above.

After two rain-soaked days, patches of blue sky appeared between the trees. Sunlight reflected across the hood of the Ram, the campground began drying out, and the entire morning felt like Plymouth had finally remembered it was summertime.

“Methinks I spy a bright orb of warmth peeking forth betwixt the pines.”

A proper Blackstone breakfast

With dry weather overhead, the Blackstone went back to work. Breakfast included bacon, sausage links, sausage patties, scrambled eggs, and a sausage-and-cheddar omelet.

Then Sade added a new chapter to Trail Tater culinary history by creating the first-ever Trail Tater banana omelet. Whether it becomes a treasured tradition or remains a legendary Plymouth experiment, Sade can forever claim the honor of inventing it.

Day Three · Massachusetts joins the map

A Trail Tater tradition continues

With the sun finally shining and the camper dry, Billie was able to do something she had been waiting to do since we arrived: add Massachusetts to Trail Tater's travel map.

Massachusetts now joins Connecticut, the place where every Trail Tater journey begins, as the second state on the map.

One small detail made this sticker especially fitting for the Plymouth adventure. Sailing across Massachusetts is a familiar ship—the Mayflower. Some stickers simply mark where we have traveled. This one marks where part of our family's story began.

Day Three · Afternoon

Pilgrim Hall Museum

This afternoon became something far more personal than a museum visit.

Walking through Pilgrim Hall Museum was not simply a matter of looking at old objects behind glass. It felt like stepping into the world our family came from.

In the museum's first gallery, photography was not permitted. One display focused on the servants of Plymouth Colony, and only two men were given individual descriptions: John Howland and George Soule. Knowing that both are our direct ancestors transformed that display from history into family.

Nearby, it was impossible not to think of Elizabeth Tilley. She crossed the Atlantic as a young girl, lost both of her parents during the first terrible winter, and later married John Howland. Her courage and endurance helped shape generations that followed.

Hands across four centuries

There are photographs that record where you went. Then there are photographs that record what you felt.

At Pilgrim Hall Museum, every member of the Trail Tater crew placed a hand on an authentic fragment of Plymouth Rock at the same time. For most visitors, it is an important piece of American history. For us, it was something more.

More than four centuries ago, John Howland, Elizabeth Tilley, and George Soule arrived in this place carrying hope, uncertainty, and the determination to begin again. Generations later, their descendants stood together and reached out to touch a piece of that same story.

Eight hands. One family. Four centuries apart, yet connected by a single stone.

The human side of history

The museum's furniture, books, chests, tools, and household objects made colonial life feel real rather than distant. These were not royal treasures. They were practical belongings used by people building new lives in an unfamiliar place.

Billie spotted an original early gravestone displayed indoors and immediately pointed it out to me. For a cemetery superintendent, that was one of the museum's quiet highlights. It was also a reminder of how much cemetery history she has absorbed simply by exploring old burying grounds with me.

Day Three · Plymouth Waterfront

From the museum to the waterfront

Billie later shared three photographs from our walk along Plymouth's waterfront: the masts of Mayflower II rising above the parking lot, the old Plymouth Rock marker, and the famous stone itself beneath the portico.

Seeing the museum's touchable fragment first and the waterfront rock later tied the afternoon together. One was a moment we could share with our hands; the other was the landmark that generations have traveled to see.

What Plymouth Rock means to us

Whether Plymouth Rock marks the exact place where the Pilgrims first stepped ashore matters less than what it has come to represent.

It stands for hope carried across an ocean, for families willing to risk everything in search of a better future, and for the quiet courage required to begin again in an unfamiliar land.

For our family, the rock is more than a landmark. It is a tangible link to the place where the journeys of John Howland, Elizabeth Tilley, and George Soule became part of the story that, generations later, led to all of us standing there together.

History is not only found in books. Sometimes it is found beneath the hands of those who have come home to remember.

Day Three · Evening

Blue eyes for the night watch

After a full afternoon surrounded by Plymouth history, the Trail Tater crew returned to Sandy Pond for an easy campground supper. Three large pizzas from the campground general store—only $9.99 each—made dinner wonderfully simple before Tracy and the girls headed out for a haunted tour.

Back at the campsite, the crew chose blue for Trail Tater’s eyes. Glowing through the front window and reflected across the hood of the truck, the lights gave our little camper the look of a friendly night watchman keeping an eye on camp beneath the pines.

Day Four · Saturday, August 1

A clear morning around Sandy Pond

Last night brought a brief downpour followed by intermittent showers, but by morning the clouds had moved on and the campground was drying beneath blue sky and sunshine.
